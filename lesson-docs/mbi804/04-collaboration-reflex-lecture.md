## 1. Purpose & learning objectives

A slide-lecture teaching project conflict and communication management through 45 real, anonymized classroom-submitted conflict stories (the same dataset later reused/extended by the `Conflict Swap` classroom activity). Rather than teaching Thomas-Kilmann's five conflict-handling modes as an abstract taxonomy, the lecture walks through real stories tagged to each mode, builds toward a single empirical "finding" (75% of student peer-reviewers over-prescribe "collaborating" regardless of the actual conflict, a phenomenon the lecture names "the reflex"), and closes with a concrete prevention playbook and a paired discussion activity. Explicit framing on the title slide: "Conflict & communication management — taught through forty-five real conflicts. Every story in this lecture actually happened. All names are invented."

## 2. Full content

The deck is 44 slides (`<section class="slide">`), advanced by click (left/right thirds of screen) or arrow keys, with an optional speaker-notes overlay (press `N`) carrying a `data-notes` string per slide — presenter guidance not shown to the audience by default. A progress bar and "n / 44" counter run along the top/bottom. Slide numbers below correspond to the file's own comment numbering.

1. **Title** — "The Collaboration Reflex" / "Conflict & communication management — taught through forty-five real conflicts." / "Every story in this lecture actually happened. All names are invented."
2. **Hook — How this lecture was made** — origin story: the instructor once asked a class to anonymously write a real conflict they lived through, then had it analyzed cold by a stranger classmate (root cause, stakeholders, mode used, mode that should have been used). "Forty-five stories came back. Today you get the best of what they taught us."
3. **The Range — What came back** — a rapid-fire list establishing conflict skills aren't just "group work skills": an audit room shouting match, a warehouse roster war, a fried-chicken kitchen at peak hour, a bank days before a risky release, a construction site with an idle crew, a German supplier refusing to show evidence, two couples sharing a flat, one family ultimatum.
4. **Roadmap — Today** — 4 cards: "The theories" (why projects breed conflict · five conflict modes · positions vs. interests · communication as a system), "The stories" (six named stories to come), "The reflex" (the 75%-collaborate finding, to be unlearned), "The goal" (two closing questions + eight prevention habits).
5-6. **Story 1 — The Silent Rescuer (Wei)** — an experienced postgrad teammate absorbs two teammates' missed work rather than confront them; keeps records "as insurance"; project graded well but Wei's own words: "Everyone remained polite, but there was little trust or collaboration afterwards."
7. **Discussion — "Talk to your neighbour" (2 min)** — "Wei got the grade. Was he right? What would *you* have done — specifically — in week one?" (rejects "I'd communicate better" as a non-answer).
8. **Theory 1 — The nature of project conflict** — cites Thamhain & Wilemon (1975): conflict peaks over schedules/priorities/resources, not personality; Tuckman (1965): "storming" is a normal team stage. In-dataset stat callout: deadlines & delays are trigger #1, whose-goal-wins is #2, pure personality clashes are rare.
9. **Two kinds of conflict (functional vs. dysfunctional)** — paired case studies: Priya's factory (a materials shortage exposes cross-departmental blame; an ops manager reframes from "who's at fault" to "what do we do now," fixing both the crisis and the underlying forecasting process — functional) vs. Marcus's report (a sleeping employee is managed via formal write-up to resignation, with nobody ever learning why he was sleeping — dysfunctional). Line: "Functional conflict surfaces information. Dysfunctional conflict destroys it."
10. **Moment (dark slide)** — quote from a student: "The conflict was scheduled before we ever met." — reframing: the collision (deadlines, KPIs, unwritten ownership) is structural; only the ending is chosen.
11. **Theory 2 — Thomas-Kilmann conflict modes** — cites Thomas & Kilmann (1974). An inline SVG 2×2-plus-center diagram plotting five modes on assertiveness (my goal) × cooperativeness (your goal) axes: Competing ("I win, you lose"), Collaborating ("we both win — slow, powerful"), Compromising ("we both give a little," center), Avoiding ("nobody engages"), Accommodating ("you win, I yield").
12. **Competing story — Sanjay** — an internal auditor's findings are publicly dismissed by the CEO as "false allegations"; Sanjay doesn't soften or split the difference, walks the room through evidence built from management's own reports, and wins. Line: "Splitting the difference on the truth is not a virtue."
13. **Collaborating story — Maya & Josh** — Josh silently rewrites Maya's slide deck overnight before a deadline; a phone call (not a text war) surfaces that Maya's real issue was being excluded from the decision, not the design itself; they blend both designs into something better than either original.
14. **Compromising story — Chen** — a two-year supplier ships defective goods and refuses all evidence requests; three weeks of argument goes nowhere; they settle on a 50/50 cost split with no facts ever established. Line: "the real question was never answered. It waits in the next shipment."
15. **Avoiding + Accommodating (paired cards)** — Avoiding: revisits Wei, with the rule "avoiding is a strategy when it has a return date." Accommodating: Lin yields to family pressure to finish a degree she'd lost interest in — grateful years later, yet in the same breath calls it "an unresolved emotional issue."
16. **Moment — "The five modes are a menu, not a ladder"** — no mode is "best"; three diagnostic questions: stakes for task *and* relationship, does the other side have any reason to engage, how much time is there really.
17. **The scoreboard** — an inline SVG bar chart coding the outcomes of all 45 stories by mode: Collaborating → 16 intact relationships (no failures); Compromising → 5 intact / 1 damaged; Competing → 1 intact / 1 damaged / 4 failed; Avoiding/Accommodating → 4 damaged / 2 failed. Explicit caveat: small sample, self-reported, one coder — but "every damaged or broken ending sits in the competing or avoiding rows."
18-20. **Story 2 — The Deleted Group Chat (Dev)** — a "generous" team leader cuts an underperforming staffer's hours based on CCTV evidence the team never saw; the group chat turns hostile; Dev goes "completely silent," then (advised from above) deletes the group chat entirely, leaving only an official channel where senior management sits. Sales/numbers hold, the noise stops — but Dev's own closing line: "One thing I have ignored — whether they are working with satisfaction or not." Moment slide: "Silence was mistaken for resolution."
21-22. **Story 3 — "Voluntary" Overtime (Nadia)** — a boss under deadline pressure treats officially-voluntary overtime as mandatory, publicly needling Nadia after she declines once, reframing it as a loyalty test. Nadia escalates to HR with dated, specific examples; HR can't prove intent but issues a written team-wide reminder; the pressure stops but the relationship becomes "more formal, less friendly... until I left that team." Lesson: escalating was right, but competing spends relationship capital, win or lose.
23. **The Reflex (the lecture's namesake finding)** — 40 peer analyses of the 45 stories: 75% prescribed "collaborating" as the better mode *regardless of the actual conflict*; recommended competing: zero; recommended avoiding: one. Examples of misapplied advice given: a boss violating written policy ("collaborate"), a department with zero incentive to bend ("collaborate"), a dispute already sensibly settled at 50/50 ("should have collaborated"). Line: "These were experienced professionals. The reflex lives in all of us."
24. **When collaboration is wrong (four traps)** — cards: "No shared incentive" (a finance-vs-sales standoff fixed by rules/dashboards, not a workshop), "A rule is being broken" (Nadia's overtime — collaborating over a violation hands the powerful party a discount), "The other side refuses the table" (Sanjay's CEO — collaboration is an offer that can be declined), "Good enough already happened" (Chen's 50/50 — perfection has a price).
25. **Moment — "An answer that fits every question has stopped being an answer"** — collaboration's entry conditions: overlapping interests, willingness on both sides, and time; missing one, name it and pick a different mode.
26-27. **Resolved vs. Repaired** — a 2×2 SVG quadrant (task sorted? × would they willingly work together again?): "The real wins" (24 stories, both axes yes), "Polite but dead" (6, task sorted but trust gone), "Open wounds" (4+, task not sorted, splits/resignations/standoffs), "Still friends, still stuck" (rare, goodwill without progress). Anecdote: one student's family conflict was graded "resolved perfectly" by her peer reviewer while she herself called it "still unresolved inside me." Moment slide: "A ceasefire is not a peace."
28. **Repair story — Tan** — 48 hours before a deadline, a teammate goes silent with half a dataset delivered; Tan publicly calls her out in the group chat, she shuts down defensively; hours later Tan sends a *private* apology for his tone (not the substance) — this single move surfaces two hidden deadlines she'd been juggling; they rescue the submission overnight, then install a lasting working agreement (check-ins every two days) — "No conflicts on any project after that."
29. **Theory 3 — Fisher & Ury, *Getting to Yes*** — positions ("what they say they want") vs. interests ("why they want it"); an inline "iceberg" SVG diagram (position above the waterline, interests — respect, certainty, inclusion, money, time, credit, fear of looking replaceable — below). Ties back to Maya & Josh: positions incompatible by construction, interests fully satisfiable and were.
30-32. **Story 4 — The Intern and the Gatekeeper (Ravi)** — a slightly-senior fellow intern keeps rejecting Ravi's code for unwritten, sometimes self-contradictory style preferences; at position level ("your code needs changing" vs. "my code is fine") the fight is unwinnable. Two masterstrokes: (1) a private, non-accusatory 10-minute conversation surfaces that most of it was personal habit, not team standard; (2) they go to the supervisor *together* asking for a written style guide — a process request, not an accusation. "The gatekeeper became something closer to a mentor." Moment slide: "Escalate the problem, not the person."
33-35. **Story 5 — The Receptionist's Log (Sofia)** — a coworker publicly, repeatedly accuses Sofia of dropping his messages (he was actually ignoring his own queue); every obvious move (argue publicly, absorb it, escalate with no proof) loses. Sofia instead proposes a shared, timestamped message log framed as a protective system, not a weapon. Days later a supervisor reviews the timestamps and the coworker apologizes privately, unprompted. Callout: six of the 45 conflicts ended this way, by records rather than argument. Moment slide: "The person with timestamps wins without raising their voice" — with the explicit corollary that receipts should be kept to defend/redirect, never to humiliate.
36. **Theory 4 — Communication management (PMBOK)** — Plan / Manage / Monitor, stripped of jargon ("Sent is not understood. Silence is never consent."); an SVG diagram of a 5-person fully-connected communication-channel graph (n(n-1)/2 channels) noting 10 people = 45 channels, "every unmanaged channel breeds 'I assumed you knew.'" Cites PMI's *PMBOK Guide* (7th ed., 2021).
37-38. **Story 6 — The Bank Release (Dilshan)** — four teams (digital banking wants to ship, IT support wants to delay over UAT defects, the vendor insists it's stable, ops is in the middle) face a binary release/no-release decision; instead of resolving by volume or rank, they reframe the question from "should we release?" to "what exactly is unresolved, and how bad is each item, really?" — scoring each defect by business impact and technical risk, fixing high-risk items pre-release, documenting low-risk ones for later, regression-testing, and writing a rollback plan. Ships with no major incidents. Lesson: itemize and score a binary decision together and the either/or often dissolves into "both."
39. **The prevention playbook** — eight numbered habit cards, sourced from asking the 40 peer-reviewers "what would have prevented this?": (1) Scheduled check-ins, (2) A shared, visible tracker, (3) Written agreements first, (4) Timestamped records, (5) Private before public, (6) Escalate the problem [not the person], (7) Early-warning norms, (8) Change control. Closing tip: "Start with 1 and 3 this week. They quietly install the other six."
40. **Activity — "Defend an unpopular mode" (20 min, pairs)** — pick a story from the lecture or a real conflict of one's own; name the position each side stated, then the interest underneath; choose the best-fit mode with the enforced rule "you may not choose collaborating unless you can show both sides had incentive, willingness, and time"; design one prevention-playbook habit that would have prevented the conflict entirely.
41. **Two closing questions** — "1. Is the task truly sorted — or just quiet? 2. Would these people willingly work together again?" If either answer is no, the conflict is still open "whatever the paperwork says."
42. **Final moment** — "Choose earlier." — "Almost none of the forty-five conflicts demanded brilliance. They demanded a tracker, a page, a rhythm — and one honest conversation, held about two weeks earlier than anyone wanted to have it."
43. **References** (full APA citations): Fisher & Ury (1981) *Getting to Yes*; PMI (2021) *PMBOK Guide* (7th ed.); Thamhain & Wilemon (1975) *Sloan Management Review* 16(3); Thomas & Kilmann (1974) *Thomas-Kilmann Conflict Mode Instrument*; Tuckman (1965) *Psychological Bulletin* 63(6). Plus a disclosure that all 45 conflict stories are real, anonymized classroom submissions, and all names used are invented.
44. **Closing/brand slide** — "Thank you" / a small brand mark ("Blended Teaching Content by Yasas Sri Wickramasinghe") / "The Collaboration Reflex — a lecture built from forty-five real conflicts."

## 3. UI & interaction design

A dark-navy/coral/paper editorial design system (`--navy:#1E2761; --coral:#F96167; --ice:#CADCFC; --paper:#F7F4ED`) built with Georgia/Times-New-Roman serif for headings and prose, Segoe UI/system-ui sans for chrome. Full-bleed single-slide-at-a-time layout (`.slide{position:absolute;inset:0;display:none}` / `.slide.active{display:flex}`), advanced by clicking the right two-thirds of the screen (forward) or left third (back), or with ArrowRight/PageDown/Space and ArrowLeft/PageUp, Home/End to jump to first/last. A thin coral progress bar tracks position at the very top; a bottom-right counter shows "n / 44"; a bottom-left hint reads "→ advance · ← back · N notes". Pressing `N` toggles a dark speaker-notes overlay pulling from each slide's `data-notes` attribute — visible only to the presenter, never part of the audience-facing deck. Distinct slide variants: plain white slides, `.dark` navy full-bleed "moment" slides used for the lecture's five key one-line insights (large serif "bigline" text with a coral-highlighted `<span>`), and a `.story` variant for narrative slides (slightly different paragraph typography). Recurring visual motifs: numbered "card" grids, colored `<span class="tag">` pills per conflict mode (competing=coral, collaborating=green, accommodating=amber, avoiding=grey), and several hand-built inline SVG diagrams (the TKI 2×2 grid, the resolved/repaired quadrant, the position/interest iceberg, the communication-channels graph, the outcomes-scoreboard bar chart) rather than embedded images. State is preserved across reloads via `location.hash` (slide index).

## 4. Component & state architecture

Pure vanilla JS, no framework: `const slides=[...document.querySelectorAll(".slide")]` collected once at load, a `go(n)` function clamps the target index, toggles `.active` on the right `<section>`, updates the progress bar width and counter text, sets `location.hash`, and calls `renderNotes()`. `renderNotes()` reads `slides[cur].dataset.notes` and shows/hides the fixed-position `#notes` overlay based on a `showNotes` boolean toggled by the `N` key. Click navigation splits the viewport at `innerWidth/3` (left third = back, right two-thirds = forward) rather than distinct buttons. On load, `parseInt(location.hash.slice(1))` restores the last-viewed slide if the hash is a valid number, otherwise starts at slide 1. No external state, no network calls, no persistence beyond the URL hash — this is a pure client-side presentation with zero backend.

## 5. Rebuild notes

- This file has no counterpart component anywhere else in the codebase — it is a self-contained artifact, so reproducing it is "copy the file" rather than "reimplement a pattern used elsewhere."
- The inline SVGs are hand-authored with specific coordinates tuned to their captions (e.g. the TKI grid's five colored rectangles, the outcomes-scoreboard's stacked bar segments computed from the 45-story counts) — if regenerating, preserve exact proportions since the accompanying prose references specific counts (16 intact, 5+1, 1+1+4, 4+2) that must visually match the bars.
- The `data-notes` speaker-note text on many slides contains presenter-only guidance not otherwise present in the visible slide (e.g. "Pause after 'forty-five stories came back'", "This is THE central finding," discussion timing suggestions) — worth preserving separately as presenter documentation even outside the HTML file, since it captures pedagogical intent (which moments to let sit in silence, which stories to slow down on) that isn't visible from the rendered slide alone.
- No accessibility affordances beyond basic semantic HTML were found (no `aria-live` on the dynamically-updated counter/progress bar, no keyboard focus management on slide change) — worth addressing in any rewrite.
- Content-wise, this lecture and `Conflict Swap` (03) share the same underlying "45 real conflicts" dataset lineage; if either is regenerated, keep the specific per-story facts (names are pseudonyms, numbers like "75%", "16 intact", "6 of 45 ended by records") consistent between the two, since the lecture explicitly sets up Conflict Swap as the mechanism that will keep generating this kind of story for future cohorts.
