⚠️ The Sunk Cost Fallacy

A NZ insurer spent NZ$2.3M over two years on a custom ERP that never worked. When asked to cut losses, leadership said: "We've already spent so much — we can't stop now." They spent another NZ$1.1M before finally abandoning it. The money already spent was sunk — irrelevant to whether the next dollar was worth spending. Good PMs ignore sunk costs in go/no-go decisions.

Basic Principles

Learning Curve Theory

The more times a team repeats a task, the cheaper and faster each repetition becomes. Cost per unit drops as cumulative experience grows — important when estimating repetitive IT work like onboarding integrations or test cycles.

Reserves

Contingency Reserves cover known unknowns (risks you've identified) — controlled by the PM, part of the cost baseline.

Management Reserves cover unknown unknowns — controlled by senior management, sit outside the baseline.

💡 When to use PERT

PERT acknowledges uncertainty. Use it when activities are novel or have high variability — exactly the case for new API integrations and security work.

🔑 Baseline vs Total Budget

Cost Baseline = estimates + contingency reserve → the PM controls this and measures performance against it.
Total Budget = baseline + management reserve → the sponsor controls the management reserve; the PM must request access to it.

Key Takeaways

  • Plan first: the Cost Management Plan makes every later number comparable
  • Know your cost types — and never let sunk costs drive future decisions
  • Match the estimate type to the lifecycle stage (ROM → Budgetary → Definitive)
  • Use PERT and reserves to handle uncertainty honestly, not optimistically
  • The cost baseline (with contingency) is the S-curve you manage against
