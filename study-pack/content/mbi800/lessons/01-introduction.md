## 1.1 What this course is about

Every organisation eventually confronts the same question: which information systems should we build, buy or retire, and in what order? Answering it badly is expensive — projects that duplicate existing capability, systems that nobody asked for, and technology investments that drift away from what the business actually needs. **Strategic Information Systems Planning (SISP)** is the discipline that answers the question well. It is the process of building a plan that aligns an organisation's information systems with its business goals, so that technology spending becomes a source of competitive advantage rather than a recurring cost centre.

::: definition
**Strategic Information Systems Planning (SISP)** is the process of identifying and prioritising information systems investments so that they support an organisation's business strategy, rather than being pursued independently of it.
:::

SISP is not the same activity as running an IT project. A project plan answers "how do we build this system on time and on budget?" SISP answers the prior question: "should this system exist at all, and why, before any budget is committed?" Get the SISP question wrong and the best-executed project in the world still fails, because it solves a problem the organisation did not actually have.

## 1.3 Why SISP is hard to get right

Three forces make SISP more difficult than it looks on paper.

First, **information systems shape strategy as much as strategy shapes information systems**. A retailer that adopts real-time inventory data does not just automate an existing process — it opens strategic options (dynamic pricing, drop-shipping, personalised marketing) that did not exist before the system was built. Planning has to work in both directions: from strategy down to systems, and from systems capability back up to strategy.

Second, **planning happens under uncertainty and constrained resources**. Technology changes faster than most planning cycles, budgets are finite, and the people who understand the business rarely have the same time horizon as the people who understand the technology. A good SISP process has to reconcile these without freezing the organisation in permanent analysis.

Third, **planning is a human process before it is a technical one**. The formal frameworks in this course — comprehensiveness, participation, risk registers, feasibility studies — exist because organisations are made of people with competing incentives, incomplete information, and legitimate disagreement about priorities. A plan that ignores this reality does not survive contact with the organisation it was written for.

::: tip
Whenever this course introduces a new framework, ask two questions before memorising it: *what real failure does this framework prevent?* and *what would go wrong without it?* Frameworks that feel abstract in a lecture become obvious once you can name the failure mode they guard against.
:::

## 1.4 Core competencies this course builds

The curriculum is built around five competencies that recur across every topic:

1. **Identifying business objectives and translating them into IS requirements.** Before any system is designed, the underlying business goal has to be stated precisely enough that success or failure can be judged against it.
2. **Assessing technology needs and implementation constraints.** Not every capability gap needs a new system; some are solved by process change, some by better use of an existing system, and only some genuinely require new technology.
3. **Risk management.** Every IS investment carries technological, operational, financial and reputational risk. This course treats risk management as integral to planning, not as a separate compliance exercise bolted on afterwards.
4. **Ethical and culturally responsive planning.** Systems that handle personal or culturally sensitive data — health records, for instance — carry planning obligations beyond cost and schedule. This course specifically examines the design of information systems for Māori and Pasifika health services as a worked example of what culturally appropriate planning requires: respecting data sovereignty, cultural protocols and community consent, not just technical privacy controls.
5. **Learning from real implementations.** Frameworks are easier to apply once you have seen them succeed and fail in named organisations, under real constraints. This course uses documented case studies rather than hypothetical scenarios wherever possible.

## 1.5 How the course is structured

The chapters that follow build in a deliberate order. **Systems thinking** comes first because every other framework in this course assumes you can already see an organisation as an interconnected system rather than a list of isolated problems. **SISP foundations** then give you the formal vocabulary — comprehensiveness, participation, consistency — for describing what a planning process actually does. **Strategic IT planning and the systems development lifecycle** connect planning to execution: a business case and a mission statement are only useful if they eventually produce a system. The **Business Model and Idea Canvas** chapter gives you a lightweight tool for testing an idea's value and cost before it becomes a formal business case at all.

From there, the course turns to substance: **risk management standards** used across industry, **documented SISP case studies** from telecommunications, government and multiple national contexts, and **five founding stories** of organisations whose information-systems choices created outsized competitive advantage. Two chapters then look forward — **immersive realities (AR/VR)** and **platform strategy** — because SISP is as much about anticipating the next wave of technology as it is about managing the current one. The final chapter on **systems security** closes the loop: every system this course has discussed building or acquiring is also a system that has to be defended.
