## 3.1 A formal definition of SISP

Chapter 1 introduced SISP informally as aligning information systems with business strategy. Segars, Grover and Teng (1998) give the idea a more precise, testable definition, built from four properties that any genuine SISP process must exhibit.

::: definition
**Strategic Information Systems Planning** is a formal process, conducted at a broad **scope**, from an upper-management **perspective**, over a long-range **time frame**, at a **conceptual** (rather than operational) level of abstraction.
:::

Each property rules something out. *Broad scope* rules out planning exercises confined to a single department's systems. *Upper-management perspective* rules out planning that never leaves the IT department — SISP has to be owned at a level that can commit organisation-wide resources. *Long-range time frame* rules out planning horizons measured in a single project's duration. *Conceptual level of abstraction* rules out planning that jumps straight to technical specification before the underlying business question has been settled. A planning exercise that fails any one of these four tests is not SISP, however much analysis went into it.

## 3.2 The core agenda of SISP

SISP performs three distinct jobs, and confusing them is a common source of planning failure:

1. **Support and influence.** SISP drives the strategic direction of the enterprise by identifying which computerised information systems would genuinely add value — not simply automate an existing process.
2. **Technological integration.** SISP coordinates otherwise disparate organisational technologies into a shared, holistic information architecture, so that systems built independently do not duplicate data or block future integration.
3. **Implementation strategy.** SISP produces macro-level blueprints for long-term systems development — the level of detail needed to sequence and prioritise investment, without descending into project-level specification.

## 3.3 The Six Process Dimensions

A SISP process can be described — and, crucially, evaluated — along six dimensions. Getting these dimensions into the right configuration, rather than maximising each one individually, is what separates an effective planning process from an expensive one.

![Six process dimensions — comprehensiveness, formalization, focus, flow, participation and consistency — arranged around a central coalignment, the balanced configuration that planning effectiveness depends on.](diagrams/six-process-dimensions.svg)

::: definition
**Comprehensiveness** — the thoroughness of the planning process: how wide a range of alternatives is canvassed, how much evaluation data is sought out, how carefully risks are weighed, and whether operational contingencies are established in advance.
:::

::: definition
**Formalization** — the degree to which the process relies on written procedures, explicit policies and recognised, repeatable pathways for collecting information, rather than ad-hoc conversation.
:::

::: definition
**Focus** — the balance between an *innovative orientation* (nurturing novel, creative solutions) and an *integrative orientation* (prioritising budgetary control, cost performance and asset protection). Most planning processes lean one way or the other; the two orientations pull in opposite directions.
:::

::: definition
**Flow** — the locus of planning authority. *Top-down* flow means centralised, upper-management initiation with limited lower-level involvement; *bottom-up* flow means functional management initiates plans, which are then aggregated upward.
:::

::: definition
**Participation** — the breadth of organisational involvement in planning. *Narrow* participation restricts plan formulation to an isolated planning team; *broad* participation actively incorporates a diverse set of functional areas, which offsets the bounded rationality of any single group of top managers.
:::

::: definition
**Consistency** — the frequency and regularity of the planning cycle. *Infrequent* planning is sporadic and largely ad-hoc, often a once-a-year event; *frequent* planning is continuous, embedded in ongoing operations, and marked by constant communication and iterative evaluation.
:::

::: tip
Participation is not the same as simply "consulting people." The dimension is about power dynamics and legitimacy as much as headcount: who is *informed* versus who *co-designs*, and whether engagement varies appropriately by planning phase and stakeholder type. A plan that surveys staff after the decisions are made has low participation regardless of how many people were surveyed.
:::

::: tip
Consistency is not just "how often do we meet." It means every individual IS decision can be traced back to a stated strategic theme — if you cannot explain which strategic goal a proposal advances, the planning process has failed the consistency test regardless of how frequently the planning committee convenes.
:::

## 3.4 The Rational Adaptation proposition

Research on these six dimensions consistently finds that no single configuration of "high on every dimension" produces the best outcome — comprehensiveness and broad participation, for instance, can slow a process down if pursued without limit. Instead, the strongest-performing planning processes exhibit a specific, balanced profile known as **Rational Adaptation**.

::: definition
**Rational Adaptation** combines **Rational Tendencies** — high comprehensiveness, strong formalization, an integrative (control-oriented) focus, and top-down flow — with **Adaptive Tendencies** — broad participation and high planning consistency.
:::

The logic is that the "rational" half of the profile gives planning its discipline and accountability (thorough, formalised, controlled, centrally initiated), while the "adaptive" half prevents that discipline from calcifying into a process that is disconnected from the organisation and updated too rarely to track real change. A process that is highly rational but not adaptive produces a thorough plan that is out of date within a year. A process that is highly adaptive but not rational produces continuous, broadly consulted activity with no discipline behind it — motion without a plan.

::: example Worked Example — diagnosing a weak planning process
A mid-sized firm runs an annual, top-down IT planning exercise. The plan is comprehensive and formally documented, and IT investment decisions are tightly controlled against budget. However, planning happens once a year, and business units are only informed of the resulting priorities, not consulted while they are set.

Rated against the six dimensions: comprehensiveness — high; formalization — high; focus — integrative/control-oriented; flow — top-down. All four Rational Tendencies are present. But participation — narrow (informed, not consulted); consistency — low (once a year, ad-hoc). Both Adaptive Tendencies are missing. The diagnosis: this is a rational but not adaptive process. The prescription is not to abandon the annual discipline, but to add broader participation earlier in the cycle and shorten the interval between planning reviews — without weakening the comprehensiveness or formal control that already work.
:::

## 3.5 Key concepts and terminology

<div class="key-concepts">

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| SISP (formal definition) | A formal process at broad scope, upper-management perspective, long-range time frame, conceptual level of abstraction |
| Comprehensiveness | Thoroughness of alternatives canvassed, data sought, risks weighed |
| Formalization | Reliance on written procedures and explicit policy over ad-hoc process |
| Focus | Balance between innovative orientation and integrative (control) orientation |
| Flow | Locus of planning authority — top-down vs bottom-up |
| Participation | Breadth of organisational involvement — narrow vs broad |
| Consistency | Frequency and regularity of the planning cycle |
| Rational Adaptation | The balanced profile — Rational Tendencies + Adaptive Tendencies — associated with the most effective planning |

</div>

## 3.6 Summary

::: summary End-of-topic summary
- Formally, SISP requires broad scope, upper-management perspective, a long-range time frame, and a conceptual level of abstraction — missing any one disqualifies a process as SISP.
- SISP's core agenda has three parts: supporting/influencing strategic direction, integrating technology across the organisation, and producing macro-level implementation blueprints.
- Six process dimensions describe any planning process: comprehensiveness, formalization, focus, flow, participation, consistency.
- The Rational Adaptation proposition holds that the most effective planning combines Rational Tendencies (comprehensive, formalised, control-focused, top-down) with Adaptive Tendencies (broad participation, high consistency) — not maximum values on every dimension independently.
:::

## Practice Questions

1. A start-up's "planning process" consists of the founder deciding on IT priorities alone, revisited whenever a new opportunity appears, with no written documentation. Rate this process on all six dimensions and state whether it better fits Rational Tendencies or Adaptive Tendencies.
2. Explain, in your own words, why "broad participation" is not satisfied merely by sending a survey to all staff after a plan has been finalised.
3. A hospital IT planning committee formalises a five-year plan with extensive documentation and cross-departmental workshops, but only revisits it once every five years regardless of changing circumstances. Which dimension is weak, and what specific risk does that weakness create?

## Answer Key

1. Comprehensiveness — low (one person's view, no systematic search for alternatives); Formalization — low (no written procedure); Focus — likely innovative rather than integrative (a founder chasing opportunity, not protecting budget control); Flow — top-down by default, since one person decides; Participation — narrow (no one else is involved); Consistency — high in one sense (continuous, revisited whenever needed) but not formally scheduled. This profile is mostly Adaptive-leaning (continuous, responsive) with almost none of the Rational Tendencies (no comprehensiveness, no formalization, no real control orientation) — the opposite imbalance from the worked example in the chapter.
2. Participation is about influence over the decision, not exposure to its outcome. A survey sent after the plan is finalised only *informs* staff; it gives them no opportunity to shape the alternatives considered, contest an assumption, or contribute information the planning team lacked. Broad participation requires involvement *during* plan formulation — being asked, not told — and ideally at a stage early enough that the answer can still change the plan.
3. **Consistency** is weak: planning happens once every five years regardless of what changes in between. The risk is that the plan becomes strategically stale well before its five-year term ends — new regulatory requirements, new clinical technology, or a shift in patient volumes can all invalidate priorities set years earlier, and without a mechanism to revisit the plan, the organisation continues executing a plan built for conditions that no longer hold.
