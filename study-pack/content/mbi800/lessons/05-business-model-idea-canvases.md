## 5.1 What a business model is

Before an organisation commits to building a system, running a project, or pursuing an idea, it is worth being able to state — in one page — how that idea is actually supposed to create value. That is the entire purpose of a business model.

::: definition
A **business model** describes how an idea will create value.
:::

The definition is deliberately broad, because the same structure applies whether the "idea" is a company, a product line, or — as this chapter will show — a research project or a classroom activity that never intends to become a business at all.

## 5.2 How organisations make money

Most revenue models fall into a small number of recognisable patterns, and being able to name the pattern behind an unfamiliar business is often the fastest way to understand it.

<div class="key-concepts">

| Model | How it works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Product & service sales | Customer pays directly for a good or service | Automotive manufacturers |
| Subscription | Customer pays a recurring fee for ongoing access | Mobile carriers, streaming services |
| Ad revenue | Advertisers pay for access to the platform's audience | Facebook |
| Commission | The platform takes a cut of transactions it facilitates | Kickstarter |
| Freemium | Basic use is free; advanced features are paid | Skype |

</div>

::: tip
When you meet an unfamiliar company, ask "which of these five patterns are they actually running — and could they be running more than one at once?" Many platforms blend two: a freemium product that also carries ad revenue, for instance.
:::

## 5.3 The Business Model Canvas

You can visualise a business model on a single page using the **Business Model Canvas** — nine interlocking blocks that force every important question about the model into view at once, rather than letting any one of them go unexamined.

![The Business Model Canvas: nine blocks — key partners, key activities, key resources, value proposition, customer relationships, channels, customer segments, cost structure and revenue streams — arranged so cost sits bottom-left and revenue sits bottom-right of the value proposition.](diagrams/business-model-canvas.svg)

::: definition
The nine blocks of the **Business Model Canvas** are: **Key Partners** (what external partnerships — mentors, lawyers, distributors, suppliers — should you invest in?); **Key Activities** (what will the business spend most of its time doing — product development, sales, other?); **Key Resources** (what will it cost for manufacturing, intellectual property, human resources?); **Value Proposition** (the central block — what value does this create, and for whom?); **Customer Relationships** (what kind of interaction do customers expect?); **Channels** (how will customers find out about you, and how will products reach them?); **Customer Segments** (who are your paying customers — do you have non-paying segments too, and how large is each group?); **Cost Structure** (how much time and money will this require?); and **Revenue Streams** (how might you make money from this, and what value — beyond money — are you hoping to create?).
:::

Notice where each block sits relative to the Value Proposition at the centre. The left-hand blocks (Key Partners, Key Activities, Key Resources) and the bottom-left block (Cost Structure) describe what the model *costs* to run. The right-hand blocks (Customer Relationships, Channels, Customer Segments) and the bottom-right block (Revenue Streams) describe what the model *earns*, and from whom. Reading the canvas left to right is reading it from cost to value to revenue — the same question a feasibility study asks, compressed onto one page.

## 5.4 Adapting the canvas into an Idea Canvas

The structure of the Business Model Canvas is useful for developing any idea — **even one that is never intended to become a business at all**. By changing the language of a few blocks, the same nine-block layout becomes an **Idea Canvas**, suited to a research project, a course activity, or a thesis proposal.

::: example Worked Example — the Idea Canvas
The centre block becomes **Your Idea** — a project idea, a lesson idea, a thesis statement, or a hypothesis, rather than a product. **Customer Segments** becomes **Audience Segments** ("who is your idea for, what are their needs, and why would they find it valuable?"). **Customer Relationships** becomes **Audience Relationships** ("what kind of relationship are you trying to build with your audience?"). **Channels** keeps its name but its question shifts to presentation: "what are some different ways of presenting your idea?" **Key Partners** becomes people who can invest time and energy to help — teachers, peers, parents, subject experts, rather than lawyers and distributors. **Key Activities** and **Key Resources** ask what specific tasks and materials are needed to get the idea off the ground.

The two bottom blocks are renamed but keep the same underlying logic: **Cost Structure** becomes "the cost of your idea" — time investment, valuable collaborators, material investment. **Revenue Streams** becomes "the value of your idea" — and, critically, value is no longer assumed to be monetary. The canvas explicitly asks for **social value**, **academic value**, and **monetary value** side by side, so an idea with strong social or academic value but no revenue model is still recorded as valuable, not as a failed business case.
:::

::: activity Activity — Draft an Idea Canvas
Pick a real project, lesson or thesis idea you are currently considering. Fill in all nine blocks of the Idea Canvas: state the idea itself in the centre block, name your audience and their needs, describe how you would present the idea and what relationship you want with your audience, list the people and materials the idea requires, and estimate both its cost (time, collaborators, materials) and its value across social, academic and monetary dimensions. Compare your completed canvas with a classmate's — did stating the audience's needs explicitly change how you described the idea itself?
:::

## 5.5 Why this belongs in a SISP course

The Business Model and Idea Canvas is a lightweight tool, but it does the same work as the heavier frameworks earlier in this course, at a much earlier and cheaper stage. Before a business case reaches the level of formal feasibility analysis (Chapter 4), before a SISP process commits comprehensiveness and formalisation to evaluating it (Chapter 3), a one-page canvas can already surface whether the value proposition, the audience, and the cost of an idea cohere — or whether the idea needs rethinking before any of the heavier machinery is engaged at all.

## 5.6 Key concepts and terminology

<div class="key-concepts">

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Business model | A description of how an idea will create value |
| Business Model Canvas | A nine-block, one-page tool for visualising a business model |
| Value proposition | The central claim of what value is created, and for whom |
| Customer segments | The distinct groups of paying (or non-paying) users the model serves |
| Idea Canvas | The Business Model Canvas adapted for a non-commercial idea — project, lesson or thesis |
| Social / academic / monetary value | The three dimensions of value the Idea Canvas asks you to state explicitly, alongside cost |

</div>

## 5.7 Summary

::: summary End-of-topic summary
- A business model describes how an idea creates value; common revenue patterns include product/service sales, subscription, ad revenue, commission and freemium.
- The Business Model Canvas lays out nine blocks around a central Value Proposition — cost-related blocks to the left and bottom-left, revenue- and customer-related blocks to the right and bottom-right.
- The same nine-block structure adapts into an Idea Canvas for any idea, even one with no intention of becoming a business — audience replaces customer, and value is explicitly assessed across social, academic and monetary dimensions, not money alone.
- Filling in a canvas early is a cheap way to test whether an idea's value proposition, audience and cost cohere, before committing the heavier machinery of a formal business case or SISP evaluation to it.
:::

## Practice Questions

1. A university research group wants to build an open-source tool for analysing survey data. Using the Idea Canvas, name one plausible entry each for Key Partners, Audience Segments, and the "value of your idea" (across all three of its dimensions).
2. Explain why the Business Model Canvas places Cost Structure and Revenue Streams at the bottom of the page rather than mixed in among the other seven blocks.
3. A student pitches a semester-long tutoring project with a detailed cost estimate but no clear statement of who it is for. Which block of the Idea Canvas is missing, and why does that gap matter even if the cost estimate is accurate?

## Answer Key

1. **Key Partners:** a university statistics department or a specific faculty supervisor willing to advise on methodology. **Audience Segments:** graduate students and early-career researchers who currently rely on expensive commercial statistics software. **Value:** social value (wider access to research tools regardless of ability to pay for commercial licences), academic value (citable, reusable methodology and potential co-authorship), monetary value (may be zero if released free and open-source — which the canvas explicitly allows you to record honestly, rather than forcing a monetary answer where none exists).
2. Cost Structure and Revenue Streams sit at the bottom because they summarise the *financial* consequence of everything above them — the seven other blocks describe *what* the model does (partners, activities, resources, value, relationships, channels, segments), while the bottom row asks what all of that costs to run and what it earns back. Keeping them separate and at the bottom makes the canvas readable left-to-right as activity, then top-to-bottom as the resulting cost/revenue summary, rather than scattering financial questions throughout the page.
3. The missing block is **Audience Segments** — who the tutoring project is actually for, what their specific needs are, and why they would find it valuable. An accurate cost estimate answers "what will this take to run," but says nothing about whether anyone needs it or will use it; without a stated audience, the project cannot be evaluated for value at all, no matter how precise its budget is.
