## 7.1 Why case studies, not just frameworks

Chapters 3, 4 and 6 gave you the formal vocabulary of SISP — process dimensions, feasibility, risk frameworks. This chapter tests that vocabulary against three documented implementations, in three very different contexts: a private telecommunications company, a cross-sector study of large organisations in a developing economy, and a national government tax authority. The same handful of success factors reappear in each, which is itself the chapter's main finding.

## 7.2 Case Study 1 — Saudi Telecommunications Company (STC)

STC is a major telecommunications provider in Saudi Arabia. It implemented SISP to expand international operations, improve customer satisfaction, and deliver converged solutions globally — outcomes it could not achieve by treating IT investment as a series of disconnected projects.

::: example Worked Example — STC's SISP process
STC developed a structured SISP plan explicitly aligned with its business goals, conducting a formal IS assessment, setting an IS vision, establishing guidelines, and creating strategic initiatives on a five-year horizon. The process engaged multiple departments — managers, analysts, programmers, top managers and external consultants — rather than being confined to the IT function.

Strategic focus areas included leadership in next-generation broadband services, excellence in customer service through tailored solutions, expansion of international operations across the MENA region, investment in human capital and leadership development, financial-performance optimisation, and strong brand communication.

A survey of 75 employees, conducted as part of the study, found: strong agreement that top management actively supported SISP; over 90% belief that STC hires the right people; that the IS strategy was clear and guiding; strong agreement that STC anticipates IT and environmental change; a high-flexibility, high-openness communication culture; investment in pre-planning clarity and role definition; an internally known formal planning process; and explicit consideration of human factors and internal politics within the planning horizon.
:::

The impact of SISP at STC was improved service delivery and internal communication, a shift from reactive fixing toward proactive planning, positioning of information systems as a genuine source of competitive advantage, and improved decision-making, innovation and employee engagement.

## 7.3 Case Study 2 — Large organisations in Ghana

A qualitative study examined SISP implementation success across large public- and private-sector organisations in Ghana — spanning mining, telecommunications, finance, oil and education — through interviews with 20 senior managers. The study's purpose was twofold: to assess how successfully SISP had been implemented, and to test whether **resource availability** moderates that success.

::: definition
**Segars and Grover's four dimensions of SISP success** — used as the study's analytical framework, alongside **Resource-Based Theory** — are **Alignment** (IS goals track business strategy as it changes), **Analysis** (systematic internal/external assessment, such as SWOT), **Cooperation** (coordination across departments to avoid redundant development), and **Capabilities** (staff capacity to identify problems, opportunities, and anticipate crises).
:::

The study's key findings, organised against these four dimensions plus resource availability as a fifth, moderating factor:

<div class="key-concepts">

| Dimension | What the study found |
|---|---|
| Alignment | Top-level managers understand and align IS with strategy; IS goals adapt as objectives change; opportunities (cloud, online banking) are actively pursued |
| Analysis | Organisations run SWOT and internal assessments; subunit information needs are understood; IT-enabled process improvement (digitisation) is continuous |
| Cooperation | Overlapping development is minimised through coordination; cross-departmental workshops and shared decision-making are used to prioritise projects |
| Capabilities | Staff identify problems and opportunities and align IS with strategy; crises are anticipated using SWOT, PESTLE and risk logs; capacity-building is applied across sectors |
| Resource Availability | Adequate financial, technical and skilled-manpower resources are essential; well-resourced organisations are markedly more likely to implement SISP successfully |

</div>

The theoretical implication is direct: SISP success depends jointly on strategic alignment, thorough analysis, cross-unit cooperation and employee capability — but all four can be undermined regardless of how well they are executed on paper, if the organisation lacks the resources to act on them. For policymakers, the study recommends formulating policy that supports infrastructure and talent development alongside strategic planning frameworks; for managers, it recommends using the four-dimension framework as a practical checklist against project failure.

## 7.4 Case Study 3 — New Zealand Inland Revenue's Business Transformation Programme

New Zealand Inland Revenue (IR), the country's tax authority, undertook a multi-year **Business Transformation Programme** to modernise and digitise the revenue system, simplifying tax and payment processes for customers. The scope of the programme is worth noting explicitly: it was **not** framed internally as a technology upgrade. It was a comprehensive transformation touching every aspect of IR's operations.

::: example Worked Example — IR's transformation approach
Key implementation steps included building a modern digital system that let customers self-manage their tax affairs at their own convenience; a fundamental review of internal processes to align with the new digital capability, rather than digitising the old process unchanged; and sustained engagement with customers, businesses and tax professionals throughout, to ensure the resulting system actually met the range of needs those groups had.

Success factors identified were customer-centric design (user-friendly interfaces, self-service options), comprehensive planning (thorough pre-planning and clear role definitions — the same planning discipline this course has emphasised since Chapter 3), and adaptability (anticipating and responding effectively to technological and environmental change as the programme progressed).
:::

The impact: simplified tax and payment procedures for customers, increased internal efficiency with reduced processing times and errors, and strong positive feedback from users, attributed directly to improved accessibility and functionality.

## 7.5 What the three cases have in common

Read side by side, the three cases converge on the same handful of success factors, despite covering a private telecom, a cross-sector developing-economy study, and a public tax authority:

- **Explicit alignment between IS and organisational strategy** — present in all three, and named as the first success factor in each.
- **Cross-functional engagement, not an IT-only process** — STC's multi-department involvement, Ghana's cooperation dimension, and IR's engagement with customers and tax professionals are the same underlying practice in three different vocabularies.
- **Formal, documented planning discipline** — STC's five-year strategic initiatives, Ghana's emphasis on formalisation, and IR's "comprehensive planning" success factor all point to the same requirement this course introduced in Chapter 3 as Rational Tendencies.
- **Adequate resourcing as a precondition, not a detail** — made explicit in Ghana's fifth dimension, and implicit in the scale of investment behind both STC's and IR's programmes.

::: warning
None of the three cases attributes its success to technology choice. In every case, the success factors named are organisational — alignment, cooperation, planning discipline, resourcing — not "which vendor or platform was selected." This is the single most transferable lesson across all three: SISP failures are rarely technology failures.
:::

## 7.7 Summary

::: summary End-of-topic summary
- STC's SISP process combined a formal five-year plan, cross-departmental engagement, and a named set of strategic focus areas, producing improved decision-making and a shift from reactive to proactive IT management.
- Segars and Grover's four dimensions — Alignment, Analysis, Cooperation, Capabilities — structured the Ghana study, which found Resource Availability to be a critical moderating factor across all four.
- New Zealand Inland Revenue's Business Transformation Programme reframed a "technology upgrade" as an organisation-wide transformation, succeeding through customer-centric design, comprehensive planning, and adaptability.
- All three cases converge on the same success factors: strategic alignment, cross-functional (not IT-only) engagement, formal planning discipline, and adequate resourcing — none attributes success to a specific technology choice.
:::

## Practice Questions

1. Using Segars and Grover's four dimensions, classify STC's five-year strategic initiative process: which dimension(s) does it most clearly demonstrate?
2. The Ghana study found that Alignment, Analysis, Cooperation and Capabilities could all be present on paper and still fail to produce successful SISP. What single additional factor explains this, and why does it matter for a policymaker rather than just a manager?
3. New Zealand Inland Revenue explicitly rejected framing its programme as "just a technology upgrade." What would have been the likely consequence of keeping that narrower framing, based on the success factors the case study actually identifies?

## Answer Key

1. STC's structured, formally documented five-year plan with a defined IS vision and guidelines most clearly demonstrates **Analysis** (formal IS assessment and vision-setting) and **Cooperation** (engaging managers, analysts, programmers, top managers and consultants together). Its clear alignment of strategic focus areas (broadband leadership, international expansion) to the overall business strategy also demonstrates **Alignment**; the case study's survey results, showing staff belief that STC "hires the right people" and anticipates change, further support **Capabilities**.
2. The additional factor is **Resource Availability** — the study found that adequate financial, technical and skilled-manpower resources are essential, and well-resourced organisations are markedly more likely to succeed even with the same alignment, analysis, cooperation and capability practices on paper. This matters specifically for a policymaker because Resource Availability is often outside any single manager's control — a manager can improve their own organisation's alignment or cooperation, but cannot single-handedly fix an economy-wide shortage of skilled IT staff or capital; that requires policy-level investment in infrastructure and talent development, which is exactly what the study recommends for policymakers rather than managers.
3. Framing the programme narrowly as a technology upgrade would likely have limited the scope of the "Process Review" success factor — the fundamental reassessment of internal processes to align with new digital capability. Without that broader framing, IR would likely have digitised its existing processes largely unchanged, missing the customer-centric redesign (self-service, user-friendly interfaces) and the sustained engagement with customers, businesses and tax professionals that the case study credits for the programme's positive reception — producing a technically modern system that still forced users through an essentially unchanged, and unimproved, process.
